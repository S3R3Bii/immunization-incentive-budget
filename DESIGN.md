# Design note

## The decision and the population

A funder already pays for reliable vaccination camps. An implementer offers to add incentives. The decision is whether the **incremental** spending can buy enough additional health to justify a grant. I use Bauchi as an illustrative target because GiveWell publishes state-level mortality inputs and Nigeria has independent randomized evidence on incentives. The $12 bid comes from the original repository’s example. It is not a proposal from a Nigerian provider. The question and methods were chosen with the published trial results visible.

The starting quantity is the difference in completion between Rajasthan villages offered camps with incentives and villages offered camps alone. This is the effect of offering the program to the catchment, including families who never attended. I pool eligible children because the bid is priced per eligible child. A second estimate gives each village equal weight. The outcome follows the original coded proxy, at least five reported shots at ages 12–36 months. It is an imperfect measure of receiving the right antigens on time. [Outcome coding](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/2435320) · [Original survey and analysis](METHODS.md)

## What drives the trial uncertainty?

The original arm counts, six missing-outcome bounds and legacy prices retain their original calculations. For initiation, I use the same age band and retain a separate denominator for scars. Codes 1 and 2 indicate a BCG scar, 3 indicates no scar, and other codes are unclassified. Neighboring children are reported separately. The conditional completion rate divides completions by starters within each arm. Participation after assignment limits its causal interpretation. [Endline file](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/1581016) · [Authors’ scar coding](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/2435322)

The primary interval uses the observed variation in village totals, allowing children in the same village to share any pattern of outcomes. Its Welch adjustment allows the two arms to have different variances. In the block check, ordinary least squares estimates a common incentive coefficient plus a separate baseline for each geographical block. Counts in each anonymous village are sufficient to reconstruct the child-level fit. Uncertainty uses a village-level sandwich variance, which sums the outer products of village residual scores, with the conventional CR1 finite-sample correction and a t reference with villages minus one degrees of freedom. The checker independently subtracts each block’s mean first and recovers the same coefficient and uncertainty. [Block results](results/block_robustness.csv) · [Independent calculation](verify_revision.py)

Table 2 of the paper used a different model for contrasts than for individual arm means. The archived Stata script clusters arm means by village. For differences it specifies random intercepts for `hidno`, then `locid` within `hidno`. In the public endline sample, household codes are reused across villages. That order can group people with the same code in different villages, while splitting up people assigned together in the same village. The replay fits two Gaussian nested random-intercept models by maximum likelihood on identical children, changing only the order of these codes. For each model it estimates residual variance, both group variances and the intervention coefficient, then forms a model-based normal interval. The original-order replay matches the published contrast and endpoints to the paper’s rounding. Reversing the order also changes the model’s implicit weighting. For the specific child-weighted question, the village-clustered estimate has the most direct connection to the assignment and needs fewer assumptions about correlation. It remains approximate. [Table 2](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Improving%20immunisation%20coverage%20in%20rural%20India.pdf) · [Original specification](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/2435322) · [Re-fit both hierarchies](audit_interval.py)

## Where the three adjustments come from

1. **Single-trial uncertainty.** A skeptical prior is a starting belief centered on zero effect. With a normal prior of width τ and an approximately normal trial estimate of standard error s, the adjusted estimate is the trial estimate times τ²/(τ² + s²). Here s comes from the two village-level variances. The narrow prior uses the observed Kenyan high-incentive-plus-SMS contrast against SMS alone. The central width uses that arm against control. The broad width uses the Nigerian strict-completion gain. These are scales for a sensitivity analysis, not estimates of a cross-study distribution. The Kenyan differences are crude proportions among children followed. The Nigerian program contains components beyond incentives. An analyst could reasonably choose different widths. [Kenya arm counts](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28288747/) · [Nigeria strict outcome](https://files.givewell.org/files/DWDA%202009/NewIncentives/IDinsight_Impact_Evaluation_of_New_Incentives_Final_Report.pdf)
2. **Internal validity.** Among the Rajasthan children with cards, Appendix Table A3 reports B-minus-A differences of +9 points for falsely reporting completion and −8 for falsely reporting noncompletion. Those point estimates imply a net **17-point** upward discrepancy in this selected subgroup. The authors found no clear evidence that reporting differed by arm. For a stress test, the low case subtracts the entire discrepancy from the raw 20.8-point gain, the middle subtracts half, and the high subtracts none. Dividing each by the raw gain gives the three multipliers. The midpoint is a judgment about possible bias, not a correction established for children without cards. Using the whole discrepancy would leave little effect. If cards themselves omit doses, even the sign can be misleading. [Appendix 1, Table A3](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Improving%20immunisation%20coverage%20in%20rural%20India.pdf)
3. **External validity.** Suppose each program reaches the same fraction of the children who otherwise would not finish. Then the effect scales with the ratio of the fractions still missing completion. The denominator is one minus Rajasthan camp-only completion. For the central numerator use one minus Bauchi basic-antigen coverage. The low case uses the higher Kenyan control coverage to represent a catchment with little headroom. The high case gives the Bauchi catchment the same headroom as Rajasthan camp-only. Statewide coverage includes existing programs and the age bands and schedules differ. None of these surveys identifies coverage under the proposed camp-only contract. [Rajasthan trial](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Improving%20immunisation%20coverage%20in%20rural%20India.pdf) · [Nigeria DHS coverage map](https://health.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/NDHS-2024-Final-Report_Disseminated17102025.pdf) · [Kenya trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28288747/)

All three factors multiply the observed completion gain. Multiplication is a planning convention, not evidence that the factors are independent. The sensitivity table replaces one factor at a time. The joint result does not have a statistical confidence interval. [Inputs with source locators](data/bridge_inputs.csv) · [Sensitivity table](results/validity_sensitivity.csv)

## Converting a local gain into a grant ceiling

Let **g** be additional completions per eligible child, **b** the incremental dollars per eligible child, **m** the modeled under-five deaths prevented per additional completion or completion-equivalent, **v** the value units per death, and **k** the benchmark units per dollar.

| Decision quantity | Calculation |
| --- | --- |
| Additional completions per dollar | g / b |
| Under-five deaths prevented per dollar | g × m / b |
| Multiple of the benchmark | g × m × v / (b × k) |
| Maximum price per eligible child at a 6× bar | g × m × v / (6 × k) |
| Maximum price under the original $100 completion rule | g × $100 |

**First measles dose.** Assume that every induced completion includes a timely first measles vaccination which otherwise would not occur by age five. Use the modeled direct measles mortality risk in an unvaccinated Bauchi child, after excluding deaths before the scheduled vaccination age, times the Nigeria-adjusted single-dose efficacy. This prices one identified component of benefit. It gives too much credit if the alternative is a slightly later dose or if the measured shot count does not correspond to measles. It omits protection from other doses and indirect mortality. [GiveWell’s December 2024 CEA, Disease burden I208 and Vaccine efficacy H31](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1mTKQuZRyVMie-K_KUppeCq7eBbXX15Of3jV7uo3z-PM/edit)

**Broader dose mix.** In the Nigerian RCT GiveWell estimated a 22-point increase in mortality-weighted vaccine coverage and IDinsight reported a 27-point increase in strict completion. Assuming the same pattern of additional doses per additional completion implies m = (0.22 / 0.27) × 0.057 × 0.52. The last two terms are GiveWell’s Bauchi under-five vaccine-preventable mortality in an unvaccinated child, including indirect deaths, and its estimated package efficacy. This is an aggregate translation between two coverage measures. It can credit doses received by non-completers and averted indirect deaths. It is deliberately separate from the measles scenario; the two must never be added. The ratio may transfer poorly to a program that mainly prevents dropout. [Nigerian trial](https://files.givewell.org/files/DWDA%202009/NewIncentives/IDinsight_Impact_Evaluation_of_New_Incentives_Final_Report.pdf) · [GiveWell’s treatment effect and Bauchi worked example](https://www.givewell.org/international/technical/programs/new-incentives)

GiveWell’s new benchmark workbook reports **0.003328414884 units per dollar**. The approximate **116 units per under-five death** come from its vaccine analysis and depend on ethical judgments about age. Using $2.15 multiplied by days in a year as a donor-dollar conversion would mix purchasing-power units with spending dollars. I use the benchmark workbook directly. The funding bar is a screen for investigating a grant, not a guarantee of funding. [Current benchmark policy](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/our-criteria/cost-effectiveness/cost-effectiveness-models) · [Workbook, Main B11](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1o0l0xucYBa8rjVKwDHh_K5PZTN6GeoenaKz77bTMXa4/edit) · [Age-dependent moral weights](https://www.givewell.org/how-we-work/our-criteria/cost-effectiveness/moral-weights)

## What would change the decision?

A local costed bid, documented dose histories in camp-only versus incentive catchments, clinic capacity, and age at vaccination would do more for the decision than another decimal place on the Rajasthan estimate. A phased comparison with the same eligibility rules in both groups could distinguish genuinely incremental protection from visits that would have happened anyway. A household welfare analysis might also value the transfer itself. That would be an additional benefit stream requiring its own evidence and valuation.
